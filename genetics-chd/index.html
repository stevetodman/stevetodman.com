What mastery looks like

Six decisions residents must make reliably

This module deliberately de-emphasizes memorizing long gene lists. A resident should be able to initiate safe evaluation and management, then involve cardiovascular genetics for the parts that require subspecialty judgment.

  1. Phenotype every patient. Find extracardiac, developmental, and family-history clues that change pretest probability.
  2. Recognize trigger lesions. Know when the anatomy itself warrants targeted evaluation even without obvious dysmorphism.
  3. Match test to mechanism. Choose among karyotype, microarray, targeted testing, panel, and exome/genome sequencing.
  4. Protect the child now. Act on immune, calcium, airway, bleeding, splenic, vascular, and anesthesia risks before the report returns.
  5. Interpret without overclaiming. Distinguish pathogenic/likely pathogenic, VUS, and negative results; know what each does not prove.
  6. Counsel precisely. Separate screening from diagnosis and Mendelian risk from lesion-specific empiric recurrence.
Core correction: there is no single universal “genetic test for CHD,” no universal 2–4% recurrence number, and no permission to manage a family from a VUS. The phenotype, suspected variant class, acuity, family structure, and available laboratory determine the pathway.

The resident workflow

From CHD to a defensible genetics plan

Use this sequence for the newborn, inpatient consult, postoperative child, or outpatient referral. It is not a rigid test ladder.

1

Stabilize first; phenotype in parallel

Do not delay ductal support, shock treatment, respiratory support, arrhythmia care, or transfer for genetics. At the same time document pregnancy history, three-generation pedigree, growth/head circumference, development, dysmorphology, palate/airway, limbs, skin, neurologic findings, and renal/GI/hepatic anomalies.

Ask specifically: CHD, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia/pacemaker, aortic disease, sudden death, recurrent pregnancy loss, consanguinity, infertility, and similarly affected relatives.

2

Identify high-yield triggers

  • CHD plus dysmorphism, multiple congenital anomalies, abnormal growth, developmental delay/intellectual disability, or neurologic findings.
  • Familial CHD, cardiomyopathy/conduction disease, laterality defects, consanguinity, or recurrent pregnancy loss.
  • Conotruncal/arch disease—especially interrupted aortic arch type B, truncus arteriosus, tetralogy of Fallot, or arch anomaly—should prompt evaluation for 22q11.2 deletion. The 2025 AAP report specifically recommends chromosome microarray for any conotruncal defect or IAA-B. AAP 2025
  • Supravalvar aortic stenosis or diffuse arteriopathy; left-sided disease with Turner features; AVSD with trisomy 21 features; pulmonary stenosis/HCM with a RASopathy phenotype.
  • Critically ill infant with complex CHD plus extracardiac anomalies: discuss rapid trio ES/GS early. Evolving implementation
3

Choose a test that can see the suspected change

4

Manage syndrome-specific danger before confirmation when suspicion is high

  • Suspected 22q11.2 deletion: calcium, CBC, lymphocyte subsets/T-cell function, renal/palate/feeding assessment; coordinate blood-product and live-vaccine decisions with immunology/transfusion medicine.
  • Suspected Williams syndrome: four-extremity blood pressure, full arterial/coronary assessment, renal/calcium evaluation, and anesthesia planning at an experienced center.
  • Suspected RASopathy: ECG/echo, bleeding history and hemostatic evaluation before procedures, feeding/growth and developmental assessment.
  • Heterotaxy with absent/poor splenic function: infection prevention plan and urgent fever instructions; evaluate airway/ciliary and GI concerns.
  • Turner syndrome: congenital cardiology evaluation, blood pressure in all limbs, complete arch/aortic imaging; cross-sectional imaging when age/condition permits.
5

Order with counseling and the right family samples

Explain possible positive, negative, uncertain, secondary/incidental, and family-relevant results; document consent/assent as appropriate. Trio testing often improves interpretation. Collect the most informative affected relative first when possible. Involve genetics/genetic counseling rather than treating a commercial panel as the diagnostic strategy.

6

Close the loop

Link a molecular result to phenotype and management, communicate it to the family and care team, arrange indicated targeted testing or clinical screening of relatives, and create a plan for reinterpretation after a negative or uncertain result.

Optional depth

Syndrome deep dives

22q11.2 deletion: what changes today?

Cardiac: conotruncal and arch anomalies, including TOF, truncus, IAA-B and arch/branch PA abnormalities. Immediate: calcium and immune evaluation; feeding/palate, renal and airway assessment. Safety: do not use “absent thymic shadow” as an immune test; coordinate live vaccines and cellular blood products from actual immune data and local protocols. The 2025 AAP health-supervision report is the current pediatric anchor.

Williams syndrome: why anesthesia deserves its own warning

SVAS may coexist with coronary ostial disease, diffuse arterial stenosis, ventricular hypertrophy and hypertension. Sudden cardiovascular collapse under sedation/anesthesia is a recognized risk. Residents should escalate elective procedural planning to cardiology/anesthesia teams familiar with Williams syndrome rather than merely documenting “cleared by cardiology.”

RASopathies: genotype informs risk, but avoid bedside gene trivia

Pulmonary stenosis, HCM and ASD are the core patterns. RAF1 and RIT1 variants are strongly enriched for HCM; PTPN11 is common and often associated with pulmonary stenosis. That association is useful, but the resident task is ECG/echo, hemodynamic assessment, bleeding/procedure planning and multidisciplinary referral. MEK inhibitors for severe RASopathy HCM remain specialized, off-label, genotype- and center-dependent therapy—not a routine algorithm step.

Familial CHD: what “negative genetics” does not mean

A negative panel or exome does not make a pedigree sporadic. Incomplete penetrance, variable expression, noncoding/structural variants, mosaicism, polygenic architecture and unrecognized phenotypes remain possible. Family members may need clinical screening even when no molecular diagnosis is found.

Interpretation discipline

What the report permits you to say

Classification is not the whole interpretation. A result must fit the gene–disease relationship, inheritance, phenotype, and assay limitations.

Pathogenic / likely pathogenic

Potentially diagnostic

  • Confirm phenotype and inheritance.
  • Change surveillance/management only when the gene–disease relationship and variant fit.
  • Offer targeted familial testing or clinical screening as indicated.

Variant of uncertain significance

Not a diagnosis

  • Do not use alone for surgery, medications, reproductive prediction, or predictive testing of unaffected relatives.
  • Segregation studies may help classification when genetics recommends them.
  • Track for reinterpretation.

Negative / nondiagnostic

Not “not genetic”

  • Review what the assay could not detect.
  • Continue phenotype-driven care and family screening when clinically indicated.
  • Revisit after phenotype evolution, new family data, or laboratory reanalysis.

Counseling without false precision

Four distinctions families need

Screening ≠ diagnosis

Cell-free DNA/NIPT estimates risk for selected chromosomal conditions; a positive screen requires diagnostic confirmation with CVS or amniocentesis. A negative screen does not exclude most causes of CHD.

Mendelian risk ≠ empiric lesion risk

A parent with an autosomal dominant pathogenic variant may have a 50% transmission risk. Without a molecular diagnosis, recurrence depends on lesion, number/relationship/sex of affected relatives, and family structure; use genetics and lesion-specific data rather than a universal percentage.

Variant transmission ≠ phenotype prediction

Variable expression and reduced penetrance mean an inherited pathogenic variant may produce a different cardiac or extracardiac phenotype—or no recognized phenotype—in another relative.

Fetal imaging ≠ genomic testing

Detailed fetal cardiac screening is optimally performed at 18–22 weeks; some lesions evolve or remain difficult to detect. Imaging defines anatomy; genetic testing addresses etiology and recurrence. They answer different questions.

Print-ready

Genetic CHD: resident quick reference

Use at the bedside as a prompt. It does not replace genetics consultation or institution-specific laboratory and transfusion protocols.

1. Phenotype every child with CHD

Pregnancy/exposure history · three-generation pedigree · growth/head circumference · development · face/palate/airway · limbs · skin · neurologic exam · renal/GI/hepatic anomalies · hearing/vision.

2. Escalate when

Syndromic or extracardiac features · developmental difference · family history/consanguinity/recurrent loss · cardiomyopathy/conduction/aortic disease · laterality defect · conotruncal/arch trigger · critically ill infant with unexplained complex disease.

3. Match test to suspected mechanism

Karyotype: aneuploidy/Turner/large or balanced rearrangement/mosaicism. CMA: deletions/duplications such as 22q11.2 and 7q11.23. Panel/targeted: recognizable monogenic phenotype. Trio ES/GS: heterogeneous/nonspecific syndromic CHD; rapid testing early in selected critically ill infants.

4. Protect before proof

22q11.2: calcium + immune + palate/feeding/renal/blood-product planning. Williams: arterial/coronary + BP + anesthesia risk. RASopathy: ECG/echo + bleeding/procedure planning. Heterotaxy: splenic infection + airway/ciliary + GI plan. Turner: complete arch/aortic imaging + BP.

5. Interpret safely

P/LP may be actionable if phenotype and gene–disease relationship fit. VUS is not diagnostic. Negative does not exclude a genetic cause. Plan reanalysis and clinical family screening when indicated.

6. Counsel precisely

Screening ≠ diagnosis. Transmission ≠ phenotype. Molecular risk ≠ empiric lesion recurrence. Prenatal imaging ≠ etiologic testing.
