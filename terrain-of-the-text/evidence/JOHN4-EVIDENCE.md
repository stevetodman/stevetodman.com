# John 4 Evidence Note — Round 2

## Scope

This note governs what the calibration episode may safely say or draw about John 4:1–43. It separates first-century travel evidence, the broad Samaritan corridor, the local Sychar/Jacob's Well problem, and interpretation.

## 1. Travel through Samaria

Josephus provides direct first-century evidence that travel through Samaritan territory was not an abnormal route for Galileans.

- *Antiquities* 20.118: Josephus says it was the custom of Galileans going to Jerusalem for festivals to travel through Samaritan country; the episode itself also shows that such travel could be socially dangerous because a violent clash occurred near Ginea.
- *Life* 269: Josephus describes travel through Samaria as the route for those who wished to reach Jerusalem quickly and says the Galilee–Jerusalem journey could be made in three days by that road.

Allowed conclusion: **the evidence does not support the blanket claim that Jews normally refused to travel through Samaria.**

Sources:
- https://ccel.org/ccel/josephus/complete/complete.ii.xxi.vi.html
- https://lexundria.com/j_vit/269/wst

## 2. What the route line means

The atlas does **not** claim to know Jesus' exact track.

John 4 gives Judea as the departure region but does not identify the departure settlement. The runtime therefore draws a broad, dashed **schematic Samaritan corridor** through representative control points. The line is contextual geometry, not a GPS reconstruction or a sequence of asserted stops.

D. A. Dorsey's study of central Samaria documents Shechem's position at the junction of the longitudinal Jerusalem–Jenin highway and the important east–west pass. His road evidence is especially valuable for the enduring topographic logic of the central hill-country corridor, but much of his detailed road tracing concerns earlier periods. It therefore supports corridor context, not an exact first-century Jesus route.

Source:
- David A. Dorsey, “Shechem and the Road Network of Central Samaria,” *BASOR* 268 (1987), 57–70, DOI 10.2307/1356994.
- https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/1356994

### Alternative routes

Other north–south route families were available, including an eastern Jordan/Perea route. The atlas may state that Samaria was not the only possible geography, but Round 2 does not draw an exact alternative route because that is not required for the calibration episode and would imply more precision than the evidence review presently supports.

## 3. Sychar

**Disposition: contested. Do not resolve silently.**

Two serious candidate treatments are retained:

### ʿAskar

Representative coordinate: **32.217760, 35.288970**.

A modern scholarly discussion in *New Testament Studies* describes localization at today's ʿAskar as possible but far from certain. The name relationship and proximity to Jacob's Well make the proposal serious, but probability weights are not justified.

### Tell Balata / Shechem-related identification

Representative coordinate: **32.213611, 35.281944**.

The Syriac textual tradition reads Shechem, and ancient/modern scholarship has repeatedly connected Sychar with Shechem. Archaeology at Tell Balata complicates a simple equation: the 1960s Shechem excavation noted extremely little Roman-period material and explicitly concluded that Sychar could have been on the ancient mound's slopes, at ʿAskar, or elsewhere nearby.

Sources:
- “Die Begegnung am Brunnen (Joh 4.4–42) im Licht der ‘Schrift’: Überlegungen zu den Samaritanern im Johannesevangelium,” *New Testament Studies* (Cambridge Core): https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/die-begegnung-am-brunnen-joh-4442-im-licht-der-schrift-uberlegungen-zu-den-samaritanern-im-johannesevangelium/ADBECAB4D03C290A573F9FB6F50392CA
- Edward F. Campbell Jr. and James F. Ross, “The Excavation of Shechem and the Biblical Tradition,” *The Biblical Archaeologist* 26.1 (1963), 1–27.
- OpenBible coordinate cross-reference: https://www.openbible.info/geo/ancient/a27b472/sychar

## 4. Jacob's Well

Runtime coordinate for the traditional site: **32.209447, 35.285431**.

The atlas treats the present location as a **probable/traditional identification**, not as an archaeologically proven event site. It is plotted separately from both Sychar candidates. The UI uses a double-ring marker so the confidence distinction does not rely on color alone.

## 5. Mount Gerizim

Representative coordinate: **32.199444, 35.272778**.

Mount Gerizim is a secure physical identification and is directly relevant to John 4:20. Archaeological work confirms a major Yahwistic/Samaritan sacred precinct on the mountain in the Persian and Hellenistic periods; the city and sanctuary were destroyed under John Hyrcanus I in 111–110 BCE. The mountain remained the Samaritan religious focus after that destruction.

Sources:
- Yitzhak Magen, “The Dating of the First Phase of the Samaritan Temple on Mount Gerizim in Light of the Archaeological Evidence” (2007): https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781575065809-011/html
- Anne Katrine de Hemmer Gudme, “Was the Temple on Mount Gerizim Modelled after the Jerusalem Temple?” *Religions* 11.2 (2020): https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/2/73
- Reinhard Pummer, overview of recent Samaritan research: https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/2015/12/pum398030

## 6. John 4:4 interpretive boundary

Geography establishes that:

- the Samaritan corridor was direct and used;
- travel through it could be socially charged and at times dangerous;
- other route families existed;
- the encounter is locally framed by Jacob tradition and Mount Gerizim.

Geography does **not** establish that `had to pass through Samaria` must mean theological or narrative necessity. That reading may be exegetically persuasive, but the atlas labels it as interpretation rather than geographic fact.

## 7. Elevation profile

**Intentionally omitted in Round 2.**

An episode-specific profile would imply a known departure point and a sufficiently exact track. John supplies neither. A later version may add a clearly labeled representative corridor profile if it can be constructed without suggesting it is Jesus' exact path.
